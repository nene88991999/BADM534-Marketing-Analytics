# Pricing, Individual Reasons, and Antitrust Evidence

Public optional ungraded practice. No assignment deadline, grade weight, or assessment-specific AI permission is established by this exercise.

A manager wants to deploy personalized prices. A customer disputes the burden, and an investigator asks whether a shared pricing arrangement restricted competition. Use [M9 §§9.34–9.39](../../06_Lecture_Notes/modules/M09_Marketing_Dialectics.md), [M6 §6.37](../../06_Lecture_Notes/modules/M06_Causal_Inference_and_Experiments.md), and the [runnable examples](../../03_Data_and_Code/AI_Experimentation_and_Pricing/README.md).

1. Run the five programs and the tests. Explain why 60% benefiting can coexist with a large loss for H, and identify a profitable alternative. Keep group members, all eligible people, and actual purchasers as separate denominators.
2. Compare the demand completions. Explain why their likelihoods agree at tested prices and their welfare conclusions differ. Propose an additional price cell and explain which uncertainty it resolves. Identify the H-group conclusion that survives both completions.
3. Evaluate targeted experimental exposure. Derive the correctly weighted population effect and compare each group's expected burden. Defend a principle governing exposure; address a feasible less burdensome design and the ex ante/ex post objection.
4. Reconstruct the published payoff game (Dubé et al., 2017). Explain the small rounded deviation margin and what remains unknown about customer authority over location data. Specify a maxim before applying the humanity formulation (Kant, 1785). Do not infer concealment from an absent variable.
5. Build a hypothetical U.S. Section 1 case theory. State the alleged agreement, the facts needed to distinguish it from independent conduct, the lawful counterfactual, and the person whose injury follows from the restriction. Compute invoice differences under several benchmarks. Explain why the optimal monopoly price in another example is not automatically a lawful competitive benchmark.
6. Assume every factual estimate in the chosen example is unbiased and arbitrarily precise. Identify the reasons that still bear on whether the objective is worth pursuing. Distinguish an instrumentally rational choice from a reasonable principle, including a customer who accepts because alternatives are weak. Write a connected argument containing a supported factual premise, a defended normative principle, an ought conclusion, the strongest defense, and a condition for revision. Include a person excluded from buying and a person who would bear a mistaken enforcement decision. Explain where legal permission leaves a moral question unresolved.

A strong analysis can defend or reject a pricing practice. Its conclusion must survive the evidence and the strongest reasonable objection. Numerical optimization, a majority of winners, a sophisticated demand model, and a citation to legal doctrine each leave additional premises to defend.

Apply the same argument to dynamic-pricing communication and the data-center reassurance in M9 §§9.31–9.34. Identify the precise claim in each case, the affected people, the strongest objection, and a feasible response. Grant exact knowledge of the narrow measured effect and explain which moral or institutional questions remain.

For discussion, one person defends the objective and another represents an affected person with little bargaining power. Give both the same exact factual results. Identify whether the disagreement concerns a fact, a reason, or the relative strength of competing objections. Explain why actual acceptance, majority approval, and any single objection do not alone settle reasonableness.

AI assistance can help trace code, inspect denominators, and propose counterexamples. Check each legal quotation and claim against its primary source. Preserve actual terms, source versions, and execution status. Never invent empirical consent, a conspiracy, a damages estimate, or an author motive.
