# Chicago Price Theory and Moral Justification

A price-theory model can explain how people respond to constraints and how costs move through markets. A recommendation also requires a principle governing the terms imposed on the people affected. The critical question is where a conditional economic result receives more moral authority than its premises support.

The [13-record catalog](catalog.json) separates analytical price theory, normative law-and-economics arguments, and philosophical criticism. Seven newly downloaded PDFs remain local to Dropbox. Three primary texts are reused from existing collections. The advertising paper, market-limits article, and competition essay are annotated from their inspected abstracts or summaries; full texts were not obtained. No unique-resource total should be calculated by adding these records to overlapping course collections.

## Moral Objections and Required Premises

| Proposed Inference | Consequential Missing Premise | Course Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher willingness to pay gives priority | Purchasing power provides a defensible basis for this allocation | Synthetic budget exchange; M4 §4.32 and M9 §9.77 |
| Predictable continued use proves endorsement | The user can understand, refuse, and revise the purpose | Engagement research and stopping controls; §9.78 |
| Aggregate gains justify uncompensated losses | The affected person's objection is answered, or a justified alternative is unavailable | Synthetic compensation; §9.79 |
| Bargaining will resolve the household burden | Affected people possess workable information, rights, and negotiating capacity | Electricity commitments and canceled projects; §9.80 |
| Uncertain harm warrants waiting | The cost and duration of waiting are defensible relative to a feasible response | Hierarchical detection example and antitrust error costs; §9.81 |

These are conditional criticisms of arguments and actions. The readings contain important disagreements: positive prediction is expressly distinguished from normative judgment; transaction-cost analysis considers institutional alternatives; the antitrust argument recognizes interim losses and beneficial anticartel enforcement. School affiliation supplies no evidence of a particular author's intention or a firm's violation. The course's moral conclusions require supported factual premises, a defended principle, and comparison with a feasible alternative.
