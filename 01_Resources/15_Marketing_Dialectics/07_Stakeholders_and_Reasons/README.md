# Stakeholders, Reasons, and Public Voice

A company must decide which actions it ought to take when commercial objectives affect other people. The readings distinguish a reasoned justification from a prediction of profit, compliance, or approval. [M9 §§9.28–9.32](../../../06_Lecture_Notes/modules/M09_Marketing_Dialectics.md) develops the arguments; [optional practice](../../../07_Labs/Philosophical_Arguments/README.md) applies them across the course.

## Reasons and Reasonable Rejection

Scanlon's *What We Owe to Each Other*, chapter 5, compares principles through the reasons people could have for rejecting them. The course cancellation example compares mandatory persuasion with a feasible secure exit. A serious objection can require changing either the proposed principle or the facts assumed in applying it.

The 2009 John Locke Lectures add two useful distinctions. Lecture 2 treats reasons in relation to a person, circumstances, and action. Lecture 4 examines justification through reflective equilibrium. A factual premise, a normative premise, and a conditional conclusion each need appropriate support. These are selected applications, not a complete reconstruction of Scanlon's philosophy.

- [1998 chapter excerpt](https://e-edu.nbu.bg/pluginfile.php/314831/mod_resource/content/0/scanlon_5.1-3.pdf): chapter 5, §§1–3, especially the comparative discussion on pp. 195–196.
- [2009 Lecture 2](https://media.philosophy.ox.ac.uk/assets/pdf_file/0008/25856/Lecture_2.pdf): pp. 12–14 on reasons and circumstances.
- [2009 Lecture 4](https://media.philosophy.ox.ac.uk/assets/pdf_file/0010/25858/Lecture_4.pdf): pp. 12–14 and 19–26 on reflective equilibrium and justification.

## Stakeholder Obligations

| Reading | Distinction Used in the Course |

| [Donaldson and Preston, 1995](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9503271992) | Description, business consequences, and normative justification require different arguments. |
| [Goodpaster, 1991](https://doi.org/10.2307/3857592) | Identifying stakeholders leaves the question of how their interests ought to govern action. Special managerial responsibilities coexist with duties beyond shareholders. |
| [Phillips, 1997](https://doi.org/10.2307/3857232) | Fair cooperation can establish particular stakeholder obligations. Examine contributions and voluntary acceptance of benefits. |

| [Mitchell, Agle, and Wood, 1997](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1997.9711022105) | Power, legitimacy, and urgency help explain managerial attention. Influence does not supply an automatic ethical weight. |
| [Hussain and Moriarty, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-016-3027-8) | Corporate participation in governance raises questions of democratic accountability to affected parties. |
| [Phillips and Reichart, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1006041929249) | A particular stakeholder definition need not exhaust environmental obligations. |

The papers offer distinct positions. The course uses their disagreements to identify the premise supporting a proposed duty, including duties to people who never voluntarily joined the firm's arrangements.

## Credibility and the Opportunity to Challenge

[Fricker's chapter on testimonial injustice](https://academic.oup.com/book/32817/chapter-abstract/274999720) concerns prejudice that unfairly reduces a speaker's credibility. [Anderson's account of institutional epistemic justice](https://websites.umich.edu/eandersn/abstracts.htm) directs attention to the organization of inquiry. The course applies these questions to residents' access to evidence and to public reassurance associated with university research.

The hypothetical Harvard/Wharton headline in M9 is a discussion example. The verified HBS article has its own [source assessment](../../41_Statistical_Claims_and_Decisions/PUBLIC_COMMUNICATION_AND_PUBLICATION.md). Unequal prestige alone establishes neither prejudice nor actual suppression. Compare the reach of claims, access to records, responses to objections, and the evidence supporting each inference.
