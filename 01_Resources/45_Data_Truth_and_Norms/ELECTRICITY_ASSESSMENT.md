# Household Costs, Public Evidence, and Institutional Responsibility

The decision is whether a development and its financing arrangements can be justified to households that may bear costs. The evidence must connect the relevant project history to household consequences and a defensible alternative. An analysis of an aggregate price measure may inform this question while leaving essential premises unestablished.

## What is Established

EIA's [RECS documentation](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/faqs.php) states that it collects energy-supplier billing data after surveying sampled households. Its [2020 public microdata](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2020/index.php?view=microdata) include household consumption and expenditure measures. The target population is occupied primary residences; survey weights, complex-design uncertainty, imputation, and modeled end-use quantities require their documented treatment. These are decisive counterexamples to a claim that actual household costs are never collected or released. Public geography and survey timing still constrain local project research.

The [restricted-data process](https://www.eia.gov/about/cipsea_reporting.php) provides a potential route to additional statistical access, subject to approval and safeguards. The [Green Button program](https://www.energy.gov/data/green-button) describes customer access and authorized sharing. Neither route promises every bill component, universal participation, or the identifiers needed for a project-linked longitudinal design. A confidentiality restriction may protect households and should be evaluated on its actual scope.

In the [January 2026 company-response bundle](https://www.warren.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/media/doc/big_tech_responses.pdf), Google's letter, pp. 2–3, describes bespoke contract terms as generally confidential and recognizes regulatory review and legal disclosure exceptions. This is evidence about particular contractual access practices. The [October 2026 Senate investigation](https://www.warren.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/471703-ai-data-center-boom-cost-Digital-8.5x11.pdf), pp. 8–9, interprets companies' nondisclosure practices; attribute those conclusions to the investigating senators and examine underlying responses. Neither source establishes the target paper's donor exposure during 2020–2025.

Virginia's [December 2024 report](https://jlarc.virginia.gov/pdfs/reports/Rpt598.pdf) is an example of public inquiry into infrastructure and electricity costs. Scenario-based projections are distinct from observed causal household effects. Section 2(c) of [S. 5640 as introduced September 30, 2026](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s5640is/xhtml/BILLS-119s5640is.html) proposes federal collection and public reporting about data-center electricity use, grid buildout, and household bills and costs. The reviewed document is introduced legislation, with no enactment or effective date established. Its existence shows that controversy can generate demand for disclosure.

## Claims and Their Required Premises

| Claim | Assessment | Evidence Needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Household billing data do not exist | Contradicted by supplier collection and RECS | No inference from limited public access can rescue this universal claim |
| A complete public project-linked household panel is available | Not established by the inspected sources | Inventory, fields, identifiers, coverage, dates, permissions, and linkage validation |
| Relevant access is selective | Documented for some bespoke contract terms | Establish the particular record holder, restriction, exception, and research consequence |
| Political sensitivity guarantees permanent nonrelease | Unsupported forecast | Specific retention and disclosure mechanisms; countervailing reporting and access possibilities |
| Missing public evidence proves intentional suppression | Unsupported without additional evidence | Decisions, requests, denials, legal grounds, internal policies, and alternative explanations |
| An accurate aggregate price estimate establishes no household burden | Requires additional substantive and causal premises | Matched outcomes, project exposure, financial offsets, comparison design, uncertainty, and distribution of effects |

## A Feasible Evidential Design

Specify the population of households potentially exposed through a utility's service and financing arrangements. Include project proposals that fail after investments are committed. Obtain project requests, construction and cancellation histories, assets and costs, developer payments or guarantees, recovery orders, tariffs, customer bills, usage, credits, arrears, and assistance where relevant and authorized. Preserve the distinction between a billed amount, a payment, a cost caused by the project, and affordability.

Evaluate a design that links records within a controlled environment and releases disclosure-reviewed aggregates, uncertainty, code, and metadata. Establish who may inspect the linkage, how residents can challenge classifications, and how disagreements are resolved. Record requested fields, availability, denials and grounds, retention periods, linkage failures, and coverage by population and time. Household identities and sensitive financial histories need not become publicly downloadable to permit credible scrutiny.

Even a complete realized ledger supplies only one project history for each household. Select a defensible counterfactual comparison and assess anticipation, spillovers, changing composition, treatment-affected controls, and cost-recovery lags. No algorithm can obtain the unobserved potential outcome simply by acquiring a larger file. The synthetic example in M2 §2.31 isolates disclosure selection while granting identification within its constructed world.

## The Philosophical Conclusion

Institutions should not demand that households refute a reassurance using evidence that those institutions alone control. This proposed Scanlonian argument depends on the material burden, control over information, the person's opportunity to contest, and feasible safeguards. Privacy, administrative cost, and other affected parties supply competing reasons that must be addressed. The duty can be met through appropriate access, independent scrutiny, narrower claims, and protections proportionate to unresolved risks.

The supported critique is a failure of justification when a narrow estimand acquires authority to settle a broader household question without its bridge premises. This critique does not depend on asserting unverified author motives or inevitable data suppression.
