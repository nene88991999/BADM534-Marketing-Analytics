# Preface

A firm may be able to identify who is susceptible to a promotion, predict who will accept a higher price, or estimate which design keeps someone using an application. These capabilities create consequential choices about what to pursue and how to treat people. I regard competence in measurement, statistical inference, experimentation, prediction, and AI-assisted coding as essential. The ability to identify, measure, or predict something does not, by itself, establish that a firm ought to do so or act on the result.

Marketing dialectics examines the premises, purposes, and reasons behind such decisions. Some questions concern what happens under an intervention; others concern whose purposes ought to govern it or what one person owes another. Learning the analytical tools therefore includes understanding which questions they can answer and when philosophical discourse requires a different form of reasoning. An estimate can inform an argument without settling its moral premises, even when better data or more powerful methods are available. Treating every disagreement as a problem of measurement can obscure the disagreement itself.

The emphasis on measurable outcomes can also influence which goals receive attention in research and practice. A framework such as SMART can help turn an accepted objective into an operational plan. When measurability becomes a condition for taking a concern seriously, however, objectives that fit a dataset can displace questions about dignity, autonomy, or the authority to refuse. An increase in engagement may be measured accurately while leaving open whether it serves the person's purposes. Precision, predictive accuracy, and unbiased estimation concern the quality of an answer to a specified question; the worth of that question requires further judgment.

I place this judgment in the reasons people can offer one another (Scanlon, 1998, 2009). A marketing practice should be assessed through principles that those affected could not reasonably reject, considering the burdens and feasible alternatives from their respective positions. Commercial advantage and statistical precision supply some reasons for a decision. Consumers' interests, commitments, and authority over their own lives supply others. A defensible ethical position need not wait for every relevant consideration to acquire a numerical measure. It requires explicit premises, attention to objections, and scrutiny of the factual claims on which it rests.

Standards of evidence also allocate consequences. A demanding threshold can prevent mistaken intervention while leaving people exposed to serious harm. I assess both errors, who bears them, and the available responses. Credible evidence can justify inquiry or proportionate protection before precise causal identification, while factual claims remain subject to scrutiny.

The distinction between phenomena and noumena (Kant, 1781/1787) reinforces the limits of allowing empirical observation to define the whole discussion. A behavioral model represents a person under recorded conditions. The person’s standing as a free and morally accountable agent raises further practical questions. I regard noumenal considerations as a reason to resist treating an empirical representation as an exhaustive account of the person; they do not supply hidden quantities for a future model to estimate. Marketing analytics can be technically accomplished while pursuing a purpose that remains morally objectionable. Responsible judgment therefore includes asking when analytical evidence advances the discussion and when the argument requires reasons that measurement alone cannot provide.
